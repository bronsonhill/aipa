# Dartmouth Workshop 1956

The meeting at which the term "artificial intelligence" was coined and the field was
founded, organised around a proposal by four researchers.

## Key facts

- Held in 1956. The name "artificial intelligence" did not exist beforehand; it was coined for this meeting.
- The proposal's conjecture, quoted in the lecture material: the meeting would proceed on the basis that every aspect of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.
- Three of the four proposers were young researchers at the time; Shannon was already well established.

## The four proposers

**John McCarthy** — inventor of Lisp, the functional programming language that became
the standard vehicle for AI research, and a foundational figure in logic programming.

**Marvin Minsky** — a major influence on the founding of cognitive science. Also author
of *Perceptrons*, which showed the limits of what the neural networks of the day could
compute and is credited with stalling that line of research for roughly thirty years.
What was missing at the time was computing power, and the approach proved useful once
that arrived. Minsky also advised Stanley Kubrick on the scientific accuracy of
*2001: A Space Odyssey*, and his engineering definition of AI — making machines do
things that would require intelligence if done by humans — is the first definition
examined in [[w01a-introduction-to-ai]].

**Nathaniel Rochester** — one of the people behind the first commercially produced
computer at IBM.

**Claude Shannon** — the founder of information theory, and influential across computer
science generally.

## Related landmarks

- **Alan Turing**, *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* (1950), which predates the workshop and opens by proposing to consider whether machines can think. See [[turing-test]].
- ***Computers and Thought*** (1963), an early collection whose table of contents shows the shape of AI research seven years in: whether a machine can think, machines that play games, machines that prove mathematical theorems, question-answering, and pattern recognition. Every one of those five is still an active area.

## Relevance to AI Planning for Autonomy

The workshop marks the beginning of the historical arc the week 1 material traces: an
ambitious founding conjecture, three decades of knowledge-engineering research that
failed for methodological reasons ([[theories-as-programs]]), and the recovery from the
1990s through [[models-and-solvers]]. Knowing that the founding claim was about
*simulating* intelligence is also useful when the Chinese Room argument later
distinguishes simulation from the real thing.
